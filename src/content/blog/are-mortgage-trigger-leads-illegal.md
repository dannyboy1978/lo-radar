The short version: **the sale and purchase of mortgage trigger leads by third-party lenders is restricted under the Homebuyers Privacy Protection Act (HPPA), effective March 4, 2026.** Independent lenders and brokers without one of three defined relationships can no longer buy the leads at all. Lenders who do have one of those relationships (the originator of the consumer's current mortgage, the loan servicer, or certain depository institutions) still can.

That is the rule, in 90 words. Everything below is the detail a loan officer running a book in late 2026 actually needs.

## What a trigger lead is, and what the market looked like before March 4

A mortgage trigger lead is the prescreened consumer report a credit bureau sells when a lender pulls a consumer's credit for a new mortgage. Before HPPA, the credit bureaus made that report available to competing lenders within 24 hours of the original pull. A consumer who sat down with one loan officer and signed a credit-pull authorization could have ten to thirty competing lenders calling or texting them inside a day.

The industry around that stream was large. Independent mortgage brokers paid between $0.75 and $3.00 per lead and built pipelines around the volume. Consumer complaint volumes about unwanted mortgage solicitation ran in the tens of millions per year. Congress had been circling the problem for a decade. HPPA (H.R. 2808) is what passed.

## What HPPA changed, in one paragraph

HPPA amended the Fair Credit Reporting Act to narrow the permissible-purpose basis on which credit bureaus can furnish prescreened mortgage reports. The statute took effect on March 4, 2026 (some sources cite March 5; the statute is ninety days after enactment, which is March 4). After that date, a credit bureau may furnish a mortgage trigger lead only to a lender that holds one of three relationships with the consumer: originator of the current mortgage, servicer of record, or a depository institution with an existing account relationship. Everyone else is blocked.

## Who can still buy trigger leads in 2026

Three categories of lender still have access:

1. **The originator of the consumer's current mortgage.** The lender who closed the borrower's current loan can continue to monitor the borrower's credit activity under the existing-relationship basis. Most depository lenders fall here on the loans they originated.
2. **The servicer of the consumer's current mortgage.** The servicer sees payoff requests, portal activity, escrow analysis, and extra-principal patterns directly. HPPA does not disturb that first-party stream.
3. **Certain depository institutions with an existing account relationship.** A bank with a checking, savings, or deposit relationship with the consumer can continue to monitor and market under FCRA's existing-relationship basis.

For everyone else (independent mortgage brokers, IMBs without servicing-retained books, credit unions without a deposit account with the consumer) the stream is closed.

## Can loan officers still get trigger leads on their own past clients?

Yes. HPPA restricts third-party purchase of credit-bureau trigger leads. It does not restrict a loan officer or lender from monitoring their own past-client book, since that borrower data is held under the lender's existing originator relationship under GLBA and FCRA. Past-client intelligence software like [LO Radar](/) operates on this lawful first-party data and is structurally compliant with HPPA by construction.

The semantic distinction matters. A "trigger lead" in the pre-HPPA sense was a credit-bureau prescreened report sold to competing lenders. A "trigger" in the post-HPPA sense is any analytical signal (rate-drop opportunity, ARM reset proximity, HELOC headroom, anniversary year, credit-improvement event) that the loan officer derives from data they already hold. The second is not a trigger lead; it is first-party analysis, and it is everywhere.

## Are there penalties for buying trigger leads in violation of HPPA?

Enforcement runs through the Fair Credit Reporting Act's existing penalty framework. Civil penalties for willful FCRA violations run up to $1,000 per violation plus punitive damages, with class actions as a realistic risk for lenders at scale. The CFPB, the FTC, and state attorneys general each have authority. The GAO published GAO-27-108874 on November 7, 2026, documenting the first eight months of HPPA implementation; consumer complaint volumes dropped sharply and a small number of enforcement actions have been publicized through the normal channels.

## What replaces the pre-HPPA trigger lead stream

Three lawful sources remain for an individual loan officer:

- **Your own past-client book.** Every loan you originated is a borrower you already have an FCRA permissible-purpose relationship with. Rate-drop windows, ARM resets, HELOC and cash-out opportunities, anniversary touches, and credit-improvement events are all actionable from data you already hold. This is the single largest source of post-HPPA activity for an independent LO.
- **Your lender's or servicer's portfolio.** If you originate for a bank that services its own loans, you have access to the full servicing signal set under the existing-relationship exception: payoff quotes viewed, autopay cancellations, escrow changes, address updates.
- **Borrower-initiated opt-ins.** A past borrower (or any homeowner) who opts in to "text me when I can save $300 a month" or "when my equity passes $150,000" gives you the strongest possible consent record: timestamped, one-to-one, in the borrower's own words.

## How to opt out of mortgage trigger leads as a consumer

A separate question, worth including because borrowers ask loan officers about it constantly. A consumer can opt out of prescreened credit offers from all three bureaus for five years through the industry opt-out portal at optoutprescreen.com (1-888-5-OPT-OUT). After HPPA, the practical effect for mortgage specifically is smaller than it was pre-HPPA (the universe of lenders who can access those prescreened reports for mortgage is now tiny), but the opt-out still forecloses the remaining access and is a service-first thing to include in a loan officer's post-close follow-up.
